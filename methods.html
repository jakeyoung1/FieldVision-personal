How FieldVision works, and where it stops working

FieldVision turns a scout's notes into a graded report where every grade points back to the words and numbers behind it. This page covers who it is for, where its data comes from, how matching and confidence work, what the evaluations measured, and what the system cannot do.

Who it's for

FieldVision was built for Coach Costanza's baseball staff at Saint Mary's College of California. In an April 2, 2026 meeting the staff described their workflow:

  • Scouting notes are written by hand, on paper or an iPad, and saved to files. There is no paid scouting software.
  • Trackman data is available, and an analytics staffer turns it into visuals for players. Synergy and 643 are used to build reports.
  • They want to upload notes in bulk, compare a recruit against a talent pool and his high school numbers, filter for the top players, and have AI help interpret the data.

The time cost

The capstone estimated that typing up one page of handwritten notes takes 10 to 15 minutes. Cross-referencing a player against past reports adds more, and in practice rarely happens at all. That estimate has not been measured. Until it is, it is a planning number, not a result. How it will be measured.

Why baseball first

The staff, the problem, the notes, and the Trackman data are all baseball, so every evaluation on this page is baseball. The basketball module runs the same pipeline (notes, comparisons, graded report, chat) on a second sport to check whether anything in it is baseball-specific. It is a generalization test, not a second product, and it has not been evaluated.

Matching and confidence

Finding historical comparisons

The notes are compared with every Rickey report using TF-IDF, a word-overlap score from 0 to 1. Three adjustments sit on top, each measured on its own in the evaluation below:

Only the first 500 characters of a player's notes are used for matching, which is roughly the first 80 words. The model still grades from the full notes, but anything past that point has no effect on which Rickey reports come back. Notes that open with a roster line or game context, and get to the player's tools later, will match worse than notes that lead with the tools. The evaluation below uses the same 500-character cut.

  • Shared vocabulary. Modern and 1950s terms are mapped to one spelling, so "changeup" matches "change of pace" and "command" matches "control".
  • Role filter. Notes on a pitcher are not matched with reports on position players, and the reverse. The player's role is read from the position line of the notes.
  • Duplicates. Some pages were transcribed twice. A duplicate never takes a second slot.

When a match counts

Loading…

Below the usable line, a match is still shown on the report, labeled as weak, but it is not given to the model. The report then says its grades rest on the notes alone.

Confidence in each grade

The model never rates its own certainty. Confidence is a rule applied after the fact: two or more verified quotes plus a Trackman measurement is High; quotes or a measurement alone is Medium; anything thinner is Low.

Checks on what the model says

  • Quotes. Every quote the model cites must appear word for word in the notes. Quotes that don't are marked.
  • Play-by-play outcomes. When the notes are play-by-play, every strikeout, groundout, walk, stolen base, or wild-pitch claim in the report is matched to the at-bat it describes. Claims that don't match are shown above the grades.
  • Notes against data. When notes and Trackman disagree, the report shows the conflict instead of averaging it away.
  • The scout decides. Any grade can be overridden with a reason. Overrides are logged, and revisions are added to the log, never written over.

Grades: does the model grade like a coach?

Four recommendations, three decisions

The app gives one of four recommendations: Priority Follow, Follow, Hold, or Do Not Follow. Coaches in this comparison pick from three decisions: Take, Follow, or Hold. To compare them, Priority Follow counts as Take, Follow stays Follow, and both Hold and Do Not Follow count as Hold.

MLB benchmarks

Mean and spread of per-pitcher averages. A pitcher's percentile assumes these averages are normally distributed, so values far from the middle are approximate. The raw files are committed in data/mlb/ and a test recomputes every number in this table from them.

Time per report

The claim worth testing is that a finished report takes less of a coach's time. A finished report means the same thing both ways: grades for each tool the notes support and a Take/Follow/Hold call, checked by the scout.

Protocol

  1. Use the ~10 players from the pilot, split into two comparable halves.
  2. By hand: for one half, a staff member writes the report the usual way and times it with a stopwatch, from blank page to finished report. Minutes go in evaluation/time_study/hand_baseline.csv.
  3. With FieldVision: for the other half, the same staff member pastes or uploads the notes, reads and checks the report, overrides any grade they disagree with, then clicks Reviewed (or exports the Decision Report). The app logs two things: that player's review minutes, from opening their report to that click, and the model wait for the Analyze run. A batch of players shares one wait, so the clock for each player starts when their own report is opened. When the two methods are compared, each FieldVision report is charged its review time plus the whole wait.
  4. On the next batch, swap the halves, so each scout does both methods and individual speed cancels out.
  5. Compare medians and publish every individual time. With about ten reports per side, the raw times matter more than any average.

Pilot with Coach Costanza's staff

Planned. Not started. No pilot data exists. Everything in this section is a plan, and the success criteria were written down before any data was collected so they can't be fitted to the results.

Scope

  • About 10 players the staff is actively scouting, over one recruiting window of two to three weeks.
  • Real notes from the staff, written the way they write them now. Trackman where they have it.
  • Each player gets an AI report and a blind coach grade (the coach grades from the notes without seeing the model's output). The coach's override log and the time study run alongside.

How results will be read: if time succeeds but agreement fails, FieldVision is a drafting aid whose grades need a coach's pass, and the agreement numbers say where it reads notes differently. If agreement succeeds but time fails, the review step is too slow and is the next thing to fix. The model's own run-to-run consistency (above) caps the agreement it can reach, so the 70% target sits below its 90% agreement with itself.

Limitations

  • Era gap. The historical reports are professional scouting from the 1950s and 1960s. College notes from 2026 use different words and describe a different game. Most comparisons score below the usable line, which is why weak matches are labeled and withheld from the model.
  • MLB benchmarks for college players. Pitch percentiles compare a college pitcher with MLB pitchers. They say where an arm sits against the major leagues, not against his own level, and most college pitchers will rank low.
  • Fictional demo players. Cole Braddock, Danny Vasquez, and every player in the demo Trackman file are invented. The demo files tag them with the SMC team code, but they are not Saint Mary's players.
  • Handwriting transcription is unmeasured. Scanned and handwritten PDFs are transcribed by Claude Vision before anything else happens. No test here measures how accurately it reads handwriting, and a misread name or number flows into the report. Image files (JPG, PNG) are not accepted.
  • Matching reads only the opening of the notes. The first 500 characters decide which Rickey reports are shown (details).
  • Small, synthetic test sets. The first matching test has 37 notes. All of them are synthetic, written from the reports they should find, and their labels were drafted by AI and have not been checked by a person. On it, a difference of a few points between systems is noise.
  • The match cutoff is tuned to synthetic notes. On the real notes of test 2, it marks every FieldVision match as weak, including many the labeler judged relevant, so reports built from real notes get no Rickey context (details).
  • One labeler, one source. The second matching test uses a small number of real notes, all from one outlet and all labeled by one person. It is small, and nobody has double-checked the labels (details).
  • Overall grades run high. See the comparison with published pro grades. (details)
  • Letter grades top out. The app grades in letters, and the conversion to the 20-80 scale stops at an A. It cannot express a 75 or an 80 (details).
  • One outlet's grades, short notes. The pro benchmark compares against one outlet's published opinions, read from short excerpts, on a few dozen players per split. Published grades are not ground truth.
  • No coach grades yet. The grade comparison has the model's side only. Until a coach grades the same notes, there is no evidence the model grades the way a coach does.
  • The model varies between runs. Given the same notes twice, it gives the same letter grade about two times in three. A one-step difference from a coach is within that noise.
  • Grade scale. The app grades on letters. Comparing with the 20-80 scouting scale uses an assumed conversion (C = 40, five points per step). Rank correlation and Take/Follow/Hold agreement don't depend on it; the average gap does.
  • Basketball is not evaluated. The basketball module has no gold set, no grade comparison, and no time study. It shows the pipeline runs on a second sport, not that it works well there.
  • Word overlap only. TF-IDF matches words, not meaning. "Electric arm" and "fast ball that takes off" score zero overlap.
